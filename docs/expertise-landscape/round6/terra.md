## Ranking

1. **Inscription on the cliff face** — Best fit: it puts each work literally into the rock, keeps the bench clear, and turns the front riser into a readable public record rather than another layer of terrain.

2. **Cairn** — The strongest standing object because it is unmistakably Plateau trail language and says “a person marked this place,” but it must be extremely low and slab-like to avoid returning to mushrooms or introducing a competing height scale.

3. **Tinaja** — A very elegant natural mark: dark water held in a shallow rock hollow reads well at tiny scale and belongs to a mesa surface, though repeated rows risk becoming decorative polka dots.

4. **Benchmark disk** — Conceptually exact for research work—surveyed, located, recorded—but at 6–8 px it is perilously close to a conventional map point or chart dot unless its ring and inset treatment are unusually disciplined.

5. **Rock register / chisel slot** *(added)* — A short, deliberately cut horizontal slot at the shelf edge: part inscription, part survey mark, and more abstract than borrowing a specific cultural motif. It is nearly as good as the cliff inscription, but has less immediate narrative warmth.

6. **Fin** — A lovely landform at larger scale, but even a thin fin makes every work look like a miniature building or a bar in a chart. It asks height to do semantic work that the terraces already own.

7. **Petroglyph on varnish** — Visually promising, especially as pale pecking on a dark patch, but too culturally loaded for a personal research site and too likely to become faux-iconography when differentiated by material type.

8. **Moki step** — It would integrate with the riser beautifully, but it is too specifically associated with Ancestral Puebloan landscape knowledge and access routes to turn into a reusable CV marker.

9. **Hoodoo, balanced rock, or boulder** — These read as scenery, game pieces, or accidental debris before they read as a specific work. They also repeat the rejected “thing standing on top” family.

## Top three

### 1. Cliff-face inscription

This should not be a literal miniature engraved sentence. It should be a small “record cut”: one shallow pale incision with a darker inner shadow, positioned on the viewer-facing riser directly below the work’s bench.

At mesa scale, make the mark about **6 px wide by 3.5 px high**. Draw a thin, slightly irregular horizontal capsule or four-sided cut, not a perfect rounded rectangle. Its upper edge receives a **rim-highlight** hairline, its interior uses the relevant **shade-riser** tone, and its lower/right edge uses **ink** at low visual weight. The bench itself remains uninterrupted; the work has been entered into its edge.

Place the two-digit numeral immediately to the right or slightly below-right, in ink, aligned to the same baseline as the incision. The numeral remains the identity; the incision is the place where that identity has been recorded. On hover or selection, add a narrow **brand-red** underline beneath the numeral or fill the tiny inner cut red—never both.

Material type can be carried by the incision’s internal cut pattern, using geometry rather than new colours:

- Paper: one uninterrupted short incision.
- Patent: incision ending in a tiny squared terminal.
- Shipped system: two parallel micro-incisions.
- Post: a short incision with a single offset nick.
- Note: a shorter, lighter incision.
- Talk: a shallow chevron nick at one end.
- Dataset: three tiny peck marks instead of a continuous cut.
- Code: two cuts meeting in a restrained angle.

A public item has a complete, cleanly rimmed incision. A draft is an incomplete cut: approximately two-thirds length, with an uncut gap toward the newest edge of the row. That says “not yet fully entered into the record” without treating drafts as lesser terrain or adding a second colour.

A row of nine becomes a believable band of small documented cuts across a riser. Use a consistent **10–12 px pitch** including the numeral, but break the visual regularity slightly: each incision can vary by a fraction of a pixel in length or edge irregularity while numerals remain rigorously aligned. The effect should be a cliff register, not a spreadsheet. On a narrow mesa, wrap only between distinct cohorts, never by splitting a single item and numeral.

Failure mode: it can read as decorative stitching or tiny UI tabs if the cuts are too rectilinear, too evenly spaced, or too high-contrast. It also fails if the riser is so shallow in the composition that the marks become illegible; in that case, reserve a slightly taller front lip for the two evidence benches.

### 2. Low cairn

The cairn should be a compressed stack of two weathered slabs, not a tower. At this scale, use **5.5 px wide by 4 px high**, with only about 2 px of visible vertical rise above the bench. The lower stone is a flattened, irregular trapezoid in **shade-riser**; the upper is a smaller offset slab in **top tone**, with one hairline **rim-highlight** along its lit upper-left edge. A narrow contact shadow in **ink** anchors it to the bench. Do not give either stone a circular outline, thick border, or symmetrical cap.

Type is expressed through the relationship between the stones:

- Paper: two thin, nearly level slabs.
- Patent: a lower slab with a small squared upper slab.
- System: three very low interlocking shards.
- Post: two offset slabs with a visible gap.
- Note: a single flat chip plus a small adjacent chip.
- Talk: a tall-ish narrow upper chip, still within the 2 px rise limit.
- Dataset: three granular pebble-like facets in a compact pile.
- Code: two opposing angular chips that meet at a notch.

Public versus draft is best shown through completion. Public cairns have the full upper stone; drafts show only the base stone plus a small unfinished chip shifted aside, as though the stack has not yet been set. This avoids a colour code and remains legible even for colour-impaired viewers.

A row of nine should look like a line of deliberately placed route markers, each separated by roughly **11 px** with the numeral on its right. Keep their bases on a single bench-front alignment, but alternate the upper slab’s offset left and right to avoid a picket-fence effect.

Failure mode: if the upper stone rises more than a couple of pixels, the cairn becomes a mushroom, chess piece, or miniature architecture. If every type has a radically different silhouette, it becomes an icon set rather than a shared geological-human vocabulary.

### 3. Tinaja

Use a shallow irregular ellipse, about **6 px by 4 px**, cut into the bench. The outer lip is a broken **rim-highlight** crescent on the lower-left, following the global light direction. Inside, use **shade-riser** or **ink** for the water, leaving a small **top-tone** rock rim on the opposite side. It should look like water gathered in worn sandstone, not a blue pond or an eye.

Type variation is carried by the basin’s edge and interior:

- Paper: one smooth oval basin.
- Patent: a slightly squared rim.
- System: a linked double hollow.
- Post: an open-ended crescent hollow.
- Note: a tiny shallow dimple.
- Talk: a basin with one radial nick.
- Dataset: three closely grouped pits.
- Code: a divided basin, with a thin angular seam.

Public works have a filled dark pool. Drafts are dry tinajas: the same hollow, but filled with bench top tone and retaining only the shaded inner wall. This is semantically apt and requires no new palette role.

A row of nine appears as a disciplined string of water pockets along the front third of the bench, with numerals offset to the right. Give them enough irregularity that they read as erosion, but keep their centres aligned so chronology remains obvious.

Failure mode: repeated dark ovals can become eyes, beads, or a chart series. Avoid making them perfectly circular, identical, or too dark relative to the numerals.

## Recommendation

Use the **cliff-face inscription / rock-register cut** for both published work and other writing, with the marks placed on the viewer-facing riser immediately below their respective benches.

It has the cleanest answer to the owner’s objection: the works are not symbols laid onto a canyon; they are entries made into its exposed geological record. It also preserves the terraces as the only height system, gives the numeral its rightful primacy, handles nine-item rows gracefully, and supports type and draft state through tiny variations in one coherent act of mark-making.

Method-page sentence:

> Each work is recorded as a small incision in the mesa’s exposed edge: a public trace of authorship, ordered from oldest to newest, while the surrounding terraces show the evidence that supported it.

## Cultural care

I would not use petroglyph-like figures or Moki-step motifs as the default language for this site. Both draw from living Indigenous cultural landscapes and, at this scale, abstraction may not prevent the design from borrowing their visual authority for an individual résumé. If either were retained, use only non-figurative, newly designed geometric cuts; never imitate recognizable figures, spirals, handprints, ladders, or site-specific arrangements; name the source tradition only in a care note if genuinely relevant; and avoid suggesting an archaeological or sacred provenance.

The inscription/register approach is safer because it can be framed as an abstract act of recording in stone rather than an imitation of Ancestral Puebloan visual language. Even there, avoid romanticizing or reproducing historic graffiti at protected sites.

## One thing not to do

Do not make the material types different standalone icons; the moment paper becomes a page, code becomes brackets, and a patent becomes a seal, the site returns to a UI legend pasted onto scenery.
